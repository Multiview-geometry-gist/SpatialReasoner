\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We evaluate our approach on 3DSRBench~\cite{ma2025spatialreasoner}, a comprehensive benchmark for 3D spatial reasoning containing 11,686 questions across diverse spatial relationship categories including distance estimation, directional reasoning, size comparison, and multi-object spatial queries.

\noindent\textbf{Training Data.} We use the SpatialReasoner training set containing 24,000 samples with chain-of-thought annotations. Each sample includes an image, a spatial reasoning question, and step-by-step reasoning with 3D coordinates. For multi-view training, we augment each sample by synthesizing a $10^\circ$ rotated view using MVGenMaster and transforming the chain-of-thought coordinates accordingly.

\noindent\textbf{Multi-View Input Format.} During training and inference, multi-view inputs are formatted with explicit viewpoint annotations:
\begin{quote}
\small
\texttt{View 1 (rotation: 0 degrees):} [Image 1] \\
\texttt{View 2 (rotation: 10 degrees):} [Image 2] \\
\texttt{Question: ...}
\end{quote}
This format enables the model to associate each image with its viewpoint. Single-image baselines omit the view annotations and use only one image.

\noindent\textbf{View Synthesis.} MVGenMaster generates video sequences depicting camera motion around the scene. We extract the frame corresponding to the target $10^\circ$ rotation. For evaluation, we pre-generate rotated views for all 3,587 unique images in 3DSRBench. Figure~\ref{fig:view-synthesis} shows example synthesized views.

\noindent\textbf{Model and Training.} All models use Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct as the base architecture, fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 256) for 3 epochs with learning rate $2\times10^{-6}$.

Among proprietary models, Gemini 2.0 achieves 51.1\% and Qwen2.5-VL-Max reaches 52.0\%, while GPT-4o shows 44.2\%. SpatialReasoner~\cite{ma2025spatialreasoner} achieves 60.3\% through chain-of-thought supervision combined with reinforcement learning. Our reproduced SFT baseline achieves 58.0\%, consistent with the reported SpatialReasoner-SFT (58.3\%). Mixed augmentation improves this to 60.2\%, while our multi-view approach achieves 60.1\% using dual-image inference.

Table~\ref{tab:category-results} presents category-wise performance analysis. Our multi-view approach shows particular strength in location-based reasoning tasks such as ``closer to camera'' (72.6\%) and ``next to'' (66.3\%), where parallax cues from the rotated view provide depth ordering information. For orientation tasks like ``in front of'' (53.1\%), the synthesized view helps disambiguate front-back relationships. However, the approach shows limited improvement on multi-object reasoning tasks requiring complex relational understanding, where the additional view may introduce confusion rather than clarity.

The performance gains on depth-related categories (closer\_to\_camera, above, height) align with the theoretical advantage of multi-view input: parallax displacement provides direct geometric cues for depth ordering that are ambiguous in single views. Conversely, the regression on left-right orientation (44.6\% vs 52.6\%) suggests that azimuthal rotation may introduce confusion for lateral relationships, as the reference frame shifts between views.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}

We present representative examples showing how multi-view input affects model reasoning. The following case demonstrates successful depth disambiguation.

\begin{promptbox}[Depth Reasoning: Which object is closer to camera?]
\small
\textbf{Question:} Which is closer to the camera, the tissue box or the cup?

\textbf{Single-View Response (Incorrect):}\\
The tissue box is at position (0.2, 0.3, 2.5). The cup is at position (0.4, 0.2, 2.4). Comparing z-coordinates: 2.5 > 2.4. The cup has smaller z, so the cup is closer.\\
\textbf{Answer: B. cup} \textcolor{red}{\ding{55}}

\textbf{Multi-View Response (Correct):}\\
View 1 (0°): The tissue box appears at image position (0.3, 0.4).\\
View 2 (10°): The tissue box shifts to (0.25, 0.4), cup shifts to (0.42, 0.35).\\
The tissue box shows larger apparent motion (0.05) than the cup (0.02).\\
Larger parallax indicates closer distance.\\
\textbf{Answer: A. tissue box} \textcolor{green}{\ding{51}}
\end{promptbox}

The multi-view model leverages parallax displacement---closer objects shift more under camera rotation---to correctly determine depth ordering, while the single-view model relies solely on estimated coordinates which may be inaccurate.

The following case shows a limitation where multi-view input introduces confusion for left-right orientation.

\begin{promptbox}[Orientation Failure: Left-right confusion]
\small
\textbf{Question:} Is the lamp to the left of the monitor?

\textbf{Single-View Response (Correct):}\\
The lamp is at x=0.3, monitor at x=0.7. Since 0.3 < 0.7, lamp is to the left.\\
\textbf{Answer: A. Yes} \textcolor{green}{\ding{51}}

\textbf{Multi-View Response (Incorrect):}\\
View 1: lamp at x=0.3, monitor at x=0.7.\\
View 2 (10° rotation): lamp at x=0.35, monitor at x=0.65.\\
The relative positions change between views, suggesting...\\
\textbf{Answer: B. No} \textcolor{red}{\ding{55}}
\end{promptbox}

For lateral (left-right) relationships, camera rotation changes the apparent x-coordinates of objects, potentially confusing the model about stable spatial relationships. This explains the accuracy drop from 52.6\% to 44.6\% on the ``orientation\_on\_the\_left'' category.
